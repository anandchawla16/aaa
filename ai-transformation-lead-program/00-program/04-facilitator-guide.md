# Facilitator Guide

For the person running the room. Module-specific answers are in each module's
answer key; this is about how to run the day.

---

## What you are actually teaching

Not prompting. **Professional scepticism, applied to a new kind of output.**

Every participant will produce competent-looking work within twenty minutes.
The difficulty of this programme is not getting output; it is getting people to
stop trusting output that looks right. Almost every design choice in Module 1 —
the deliberately defective data, the ungrounded-then-grounded pairs, the
mandatory hand-verification, the peer handover test — exists to create moments
where a participant discovers, personally, that they were about to be wrong.

**Protect those moments.** The temptation is to warn people first. Do not. A
warning produces agreement; a discovery produces a change in behaviour.

---

## The single most important facilitation habit

When a participant shows you an output, ask: **"How would you know if that were
wrong?"**

Ask it about good outputs, not just suspicious ones. Ask it until people start
asking it of themselves. If they leave Module 1 with one habit, this is the one
that is worth the day.

Never answer it for them.

---

## Room and rhythm

- **Laptops open, but managed.** Exercises are hands-on; teaching blocks are
  not. Say "screens down" for teaching blocks and mean it.
- **Announce the timebox before each exercise, not after.** "You have twenty
  minutes; at fifteen I will tell you to start writing up."
- **The debrief is the lesson.** If you are running late, shorten the build and
  protect the debrief. Participants can finish a table afterwards; they cannot
  reconstruct a debrief.
- **Circulate constantly during exercises.** You are looking for two things: the
  participant who has accepted everything, and the participant who has found
  something. Both are debrief material.

---

## Handling the four people who are always in the room

### The sceptic
*"This will hallucinate and we'll get sued."*

Do not argue. **Agree, and put them to work.** Ex 1.2 Part B exists for them —
have them produce the failure and present it to the room. A sceptic who has
demonstrated a failure in front of peers becomes the programme's most useful
advocate, because they are the one who will insist on the verification step.

### The enthusiast
*"We could automate the whole admission process."*

Also do not argue. Ask which lane it is in, and who signs. Then ask what happens
on the day it is wrong about a specific child. Ex 1.8 is designed to land on
this person hardest — protect its full length when they are in the cohort.

### The senior person who will not touch a laptop
Common, and usually a Principal or a function head. Two options: pair them with
a participant and make them the *reviewer* — they read outputs and challenge
them, which is genuinely the harder half. Or accept that they are an observer
and brief them separately on the Never List and the Three-Lane model, which are
the parts they will actually need to enforce.

Do not let them sit silently through exercises. It signals to the room that the
hands-on work is optional.

### The one who is already using it heavily
Often the strongest participant and often the biggest risk — they have habits
built without guardrails, and they may already be pasting real data somewhere
they should not. Find this out early and privately, ideally in the break after
Ex 1.2. Do not make an example of them. Make them a co-facilitator on the
technical parts and hold them to the highest standard on Ex 1.8.

## Difficult moments and what to say

**"But it got the right answer without the policy attached."**

> "This time. You had no way of knowing that when you read it, and you didn't
> know about Clause 5.2 either. The question is not whether it was right — it is
> whether you could tell. Grounding is what makes it checkable."

**"This is too slow. I could have written the letter myself in ten minutes."**

> "For one letter, yes. This is not a letter, it is the thing that writes four
> hundred letters. Judge it on the fourth hundredth."

**"Our data is much worse than this."**

> "Good — then you already know the first step, and we have just given it a
> name. Run the defect report on your own export this week and bring it to
> Module 2."

**"Can't we just let it send the reminders automatically?"**

> "Clause 6.5, and it is a Board rule. But take the question seriously — what
> would have to be true for that to be safe? Work through it, and you will
> arrive at why the rule exists."

**A participant produces something genuinely wrong and does not notice.**

Do not correct it publicly in the moment. Ask them the habit question and let
them find it. If they cannot, take it to the debrief as an anonymous example —
"someone in the room has a table that doesn't reconcile; here is how you would
catch that." Every cohort has one, and treating it as normal rather than
embarrassing is what makes people admit the next one.

## Running the exercises

**Ex 1.0** — Silent. Do not discuss answers. Its purpose is a baseline and a
read on the room; collect Part B impressions to decide where to spend time.

**Ex 1.1** — The marked behaviour is *overriding a score*. Say explicitly, once,
early: "Two of these scores must be yours, not the model's." Otherwise most
cohorts submit the output unchanged.

**Ex 1.2** — Protect Part B. The sort is warm-up; the failure is the lesson.
If time is tight, cut the sort to eight items, never the failure.

**Ex 1.3** — The handover swap is non-negotiable. Participants will resist
("mine isn't finished"). That is the point: an unfinished prompt in someone
else's hands is exactly the experiment.

**Ex 1.4** — Watch for the grounded pass drafting the Q10 parent response. When
it happens, do not let it pass quietly. This is the module's clearest Never List
moment, and the room needs to see it named.

**Ex 1.5** — The hand-verification will be skipped unless you police it.
Circulate and ask to see the arithmetic. It carries the highest single mark
allocation in the module for exactly this reason.

**Ex 1.6** — The casing trap is the highest-value teaching moment of the
afternoon. If most of the room misses it, that is a better session than if most
catch it — the 75% overstatement on the group's largest channel is a number they
will remember.

**Ex 1.7** — Push hard on "who owns this on Monday". Most specs name a team.

**Ex 1.8** — Never cut. Never shorten. It carries the floor and the commitment.

---

## Timing recovery

You will run late. Every cohort does, usually by ten minutes at the second
break. Recover in this order:

| Cut | Saves | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Ex 1.6 academic spot-check | 3 min | Low |
| Ex 1.5 committee note → homework | 5 min | Medium — raise the Lane 2 point verbally |
| Teach E folded into Teach A | 10 min | Low |
| Ex 1.7 → Module 2 pre-work | 15 min | Medium — LO7 moves |

**Do not recover time by shortening debriefs.** That is where the module
happens.

---

## After the module

Within 48 hours:

1. Mark against the rubric and the answer key's exact figures.
2. Return marks with **one sentence** of specific feedback per participant. Not
   a paragraph — one sentence they will remember.
3. Flag anyone below the Ex 1.8 floor and schedule their resubmission before
   Module 2.
4. Flag anyone who scored well and never disagreed with an output. Tell them
   directly. They will struggle in Module 6, and it is kinder to say so now.
5. Confirm each participant's 30-day commitment has a named owner and a baseline
   date **before** their build date. Chase the ones that do not.

The commitment chase is the highest-value thing you do after the module. A
cohort that leaves with signed commitments and no follow-up produces roughly
nothing; a cohort chased once at day seven produces most of the programme's
value.

---

## What success looks like at the end of Module 1

Not enthusiasm. Enthusiasm at the end of day one is the normal state and
predicts nothing.

Look for these instead:

- Someone asked "where is that grounded?" without being prompted.
- Someone refused to answer a question in an exercise because it was Lane 3.
- Someone found an error in their own output and said so publicly.
- Someone downgraded their asset to a simpler surface.
- At least one commitment names an owner who has already said yes.

Three of these five is a good cohort.
